# Verk — Visual Identity Lock

> This document closes the strategic phase and opens the building phase.
> After approval, visual identity decisions stop being debated.
> This is the lock.

---

## Part 1 — Three Directions for La Obra

The territory is confirmed: La Obra.

But "obra" is large enough to hold three genuinely different worlds inside it. These are not variations. They do not share an aesthetic. They share only the concept — and each one bets it on something different.

---

### Direction A — LA OBRA COMO INFRAESTRUCTURA

**The world:**

This direction takes the construction half of "obra" and commits to it without apology. The visual world is the engineering office, the site plan, the structural section cut. Everything that appears on the page can be explained with a reason — not an aesthetic reason, a structural one. Every element is load-bearing. Nothing is decoration.

**Emotional feeling:**

Authority. The quiet certainty of a person who has built before and knows the difference between a structure that holds and one that doesn't. This direction does not try to be beautiful. It tries to be correct. And in that correctness, beauty arrives without being invited.

The feeling is: entering a building that was designed by someone who understood gravity.

**Typography personality:**

Condensed. Measured. Economical with space. Headlines that feel like specifications — precise and complete, no word surplus. Think DIN, the typeface of German road signs and engineering manuals: not because it's pretty but because it is exactly right. Numbers and dimensions should appear at the same visual weight as letters. The type system treats all information as equally important, which means the hierarchy comes entirely from scale, not from decorative weight variation.

**Motion personality:**

Mechanical. Things move because they need to be in a different position. Not because motion is beautiful. The animation of a construction arm lowering a beam — purposeful, load-aware, no flourish. Items enter by sliding into position from the direction they would structurally originate. A column rises from the bottom. A beam enters from the left. Elements snap to their final position with a brief lock — a 30ms hold at the end of an animation that makes you feel something click into place.

**Color behavior:**

Almost monochromatic. Black and cream carry 90% of the visual weight. Lime appears as the single active accent — the caution color of construction sites, the warning yellow-green of active machinery. Violet is entirely absent from this direction. The absence of violet signals: this is the structure, not the intelligence. The concrete before the electrical systems are installed.

**Photography style:**

The construction documentation tradition: site progress photos, material close-ups, structural details photographed for record, not for beauty. Rebar arrangements. The shadow patterns of scaffolding on raw concrete. Things that exist to be built, not to be admired. Shot in available light with no attempt to flatter the subject.

**Imagery style:**

Technical drawings. Section cuts. Blueprint overlays on photographs. Material callout diagrams. The page can hold images of things mid-construction — never the finished building, never the ribbon cutting. Always the work in progress.

**Use of isotipo:**

The isotipo in this direction becomes a structural element — two I-beams seen in cross-section from an angle. It appears in black on cream with absolute precision. No glow, no shadow, no visual treatment. It should look like something that was drafted before it was placed, not something that was designed.

**Section transitions:**

Hard. One section ends completely before the next begins. The transition is a clean horizontal line — the kind of dividing line between floors on an architectural section drawing. Color changes are immediate, not gradual. The difference between dark and light sections is not eased — it is a decision.

**Footer style:**

An engineering specification sheet. Dense but ordered. Every piece of information in its correct position. The footer closes the building the same way a structural engineer closes a set of drawings: every detail accounted for, nothing speculative.

**Hero style:**

The site plan. The hero is organized as if seen from above — a top-down view of a system being built. The headline appears as a title block in the lower left, the way architectural drawings are titled. The isotipo appears large in the upper right, as the stamp of the firm.

**Strengths:**

- Maximum credibility with business clients who need to believe in operational reliability
- Completely original in the digital studio space — no one else is occupying this aesthetic
- Directly communicates Verk's core product (systems, infrastructure, things that work)
- Ages well — structural aesthetics are not trend-dependent
- Strong for print materials and presentations alongside the website

**Weaknesses:**

- Could read as cold to clients in the primary target market (clinic owners, architects) who also want warmth
- Difficult to scale creatively — the vocabulary is narrow and constrained
- Risks feeling like a heavy civil engineering firm rather than a digital studio
- The absence of violet means Verk loses the color it actually owns in its identity

---

### Direction B — LA OBRA COMO CREACIÓN

**The world:**

This direction takes the creative/cultural half of "obra" and follows it without concession. The visual world is the editing suite, the composer's notebook, the darkroom at 3am, the director's cut. The work is not being built — it is being expressed. The system Verk creates is not infrastructure — it is an authored piece, a composition, something that has its own voice.

**Emotional feeling:**

The specific feeling of a creative person in full flow at an unusual hour. The feeling of something being made that didn't exist before, with an awareness that the making is itself significant. This direction communicates that Verk is not a service provider — it is an author. And every system it builds is a text that can be read.

The feeling is: the last frame of a film before the credits. Recognition and completion.

**Typography personality:**

Editorial. High contrast between weights — thin at body, bold at display, with abrupt changes in size creating visual rhythm that mirrors editorial layout. Consider mixing a high-contrast grotesque for headlines with a mono for body text, reversing the conventional hierarchy. The type system should feel like it was designed for a specific publication, not a universal product.

**Motion personality:**

Cinematic. The cut is the primary motion event — not the transition, not the ease, but the cut. Things appear and disappear without explanation. The viewer's brain fills the gap. Slow moments and fast moments exist in the same sequence, as a film editor would create them. The pacing is authored, not algorithmic.

**Color behavior:**

Deep and specific. The black is very dark, approaching ink. The cream is the color of aged paper, not construction material. The violet appears prominently — the color of something seen under low light, something that exists in the intelligence layer. Lime appears rarely, as a single point of unexpected brightness in a composition that is otherwise dark and considered.

**Photography style:**

The tradition of cinema stills and behind-the-scenes documentation. Images that capture process rather than product. The lighting of a photography set during preparation, not during the shoot. The footage before the final edit. Hands in motion. Work in progress that has not yet become what it will be.

**Imagery style:**

Film frames. Sound waves. Score notation. The visual language of process documentation — not the finished work, but the evidence that the work happened.

**Use of isotipo:**

The isotipo becomes an editing mark. In film, editing marks indicate the cut point — the precise moment between one shot and the next. The two shapes are that gap, made visible. They appear at transitions, at moments of change, at the point where one section of the site gives way to another.

**Section transitions:**

Cinematic cuts. Hard transitions where one scene ends and another begins without visual continuity. The jump cut, used intentionally. Color changes feel like scene changes — the same narrative, a different perspective.

**Footer style:**

End credits. Dense with information, moving slowly through a dark background. The footer is not a navigation tool — it is the closing sequence, the moment where authorship is acknowledged.

**Hero style:**

The opening frame. Not the establishing shot (that's generic) — the second shot, the one that comes after the film has already begun. The hero drops the viewer into something that is already happening, and the viewer must orient themselves. The headline is the first line of dialogue, not the title card.

**Strengths:**

- Genuinely distinctive — no digital studio in the target market operates in this register
- Directly aligned with the founder's background in film and creative work
- Creates a strong narrative: every project Verk delivers is a creative act, not just a technical one
- High memorability — the cinematic aesthetic is emotionally engaging

**Weaknesses:**

- Trust gap: business clients in León need to believe they're hiring something reliable, not something artistic
- The cinematic metaphor distances the brand from its technical capability (automation, CRM, AI)
- Risk of feeling inaccessible or self-indulgent to the primary target market
- Harder to scale — the editorial aesthetic requires constant creative direction to maintain

### Direction C — LA OBRA COMO TENSIÓN

**The world:**

This direction does not resolve the tension between construction and creation. It lives inside it. The visual world is neither the engineering office nor the editing suite — it is the space where those two people sit across a table from each other at midnight, working on the same problem from different directions, and producing something that neither of them would have produced alone.

The construction vocabulary (structural weight, precision hairlines, load-bearing geometry) is present. The creative vocabulary (editorial pace, cinematic light, expressive typography) is also present. They do not take turns. They are simultaneously present in every section, every composition, every motion sequence.

**Emotional feeling:**

The feeling is: encountering something that knows more than it reveals. The reserve of a person who is both rigorous and expressive, and who is comfortable holding both without explaining the combination. There is no performance of creativity, no performance of technical competence. Both are simply present, as if they were always together.

The feeling is: the architect who also plays piano. Not as a metaphor — as a way of thinking.

**Typography personality:**

A strong grotesque — precise, authoritative, with genuine weight — applied with editorial boldness. The rules are not the rules of a technical manual or a cultural magazine. They are specifically Verk's rules: headlines flush left, never centered. Display sizes that exceed normal bounds. Size changes that communicate authority, not hierarchy. The type system is technically correct and visually confident simultaneously.

**Motion personality:**

Deliberate. The key word is not fast or slow — it is *deliberate*. Every motion event feels chosen. The entrance of a headline feels authored. The departure of an element feels edited. The pace changes between sections the way a film score changes: not because a rule was applied, but because it was right for this moment. Fast when the content is active. Still when the content demands attention.

**Color behavior:**

The full system: black, cream, lime, violet — all present, all in their correct roles. But the behavior is what matters, not the presence. Lime only moves. Violet only thinks. Black only structures. Cream only receives. A section is calm or active based entirely on which colors appear in it. A section with only black and cream is structural. A section with lime is asking for action. A section with violet is communicating intelligence. The color doesn't just look like something — it says something specific about what is happening.

**Photography style:**

The documentation of things in their working state, photographed with genuine visual attention. Not construction site photography (too rough). Not editorial photography (too stylized). The photography of things that work — an operating room in mid-procedure, a well-run kitchen during service, an automated system displaying its output at 3am. The aesthetic: available light, physical presence, no conceptual intervention.

**Imagery style:**

Process and product in the same frame. The blueprint and the building beside each other. The code on screen and the business it runs in the same composition. The system and its output, adjacent.

**Use of isotipo:**

The isotipo carries both identities simultaneously. In structural contexts, it reads as a section mark — a load-bearing element. In creative contexts, it reads as an editing mark or a compositional device. It never fully resolves into either. This unresolved nature is its power — it is the visual proof of the tension.

**Section transitions:**

Not hard, not soft. Precise. The transition between sections is neither a construction joint (hard, immediate) nor a film cut (instant and unexplained). It is a deliberate change — visible enough to feel authored, fast enough to feel confident. The page shifts between moods the way a conversation shifts between registers: without announcement, with complete awareness.

**Footer style:**

A closing statement, not a closing chapter. The footer does not attempt to summarize — it continues the logic of the obra. The headline in the footer is the last sentence Verk says. It should deserve to be last.

**Hero style:**

The moment before the first mark. A surface — cream, warm, specific — with the isotipo already present, as if it was always there, waiting. The headline arrives. The subheadline follows. Nothing appears unexpectedly. Everything arrives in its correct order, at its correct weight, with complete confidence that it belongs.

**Strengths:**

- The only direction that is completely true to Verk — which is neither pure infrastructure nor pure creation
- Scalable: the vocabulary is large enough to hold future brand evolution without changing identity
- Converts: the structural presence of Direction A builds trust; the creative presence of Direction B creates desire
- Aligned with the name: "Verk" is the obra in Nordic — a word that means both making and making well
- The color system is fully operable and fully meaningful under this direction

**Weaknesses:**

- Requires the most sophisticated execution — Direction C executed poorly looks like Direction A and Direction B fighting. The tension must feel chosen, not accidental.
- Cannot be easily described to a client in a brief — it must be shown, not explained
- Risk of dilution if brand contributors in the future add "a bit more of one direction" without discipline

## Part 3 — The Winner and Its Defense

**Winner: Direction C — La Obra Como Tensión.**

Not as a compromise. As the most true.

**Why Direction A loses:**

Direction A has the highest trust score but the lowest alignment with Verk. A brand that communicates only infrastructure will attract clients who want infrastructure — and those clients will eventually want to hire the cheapest infrastructure builder, because the work has been reduced to a commodity. Direction A's precision is also its limitation: it cannot hold the creative dimension of Verk's work. The portfolio of a company that built Prisma (a film discovery system organized by color) cannot live inside a structural engineering aesthetic without one of them being dishonest. Verk is not a structural engineering firm. It is a studio that builds systems that are also obras. Direction A is one half of that sentence.

**Why Direction B loses:**

Direction B has the highest memorability score but the lowest trust and lowest ability to sell services. The primary market — clinic owners, architects, real estate firms in León — is making a business decision, not a cultural one. They are choosing a partner for their operational infrastructure. Direction B asks them to believe that the film-editor sensibility behind the brand will also reliably connect their CRM to their WhatsApp and have it running before Monday. The aesthetic says something different from the promise. Verk sells systems that work. Direction B communicates a studio that makes things that are interesting. These are not the same thing, and the mismatch would appear in every first sales conversation.

**Why Direction C wins:**

Direction C is the only direction in which Verk is telling the truth about itself. The person who built Verk comes from film AND builds production systems. Those two things coexist not as separate compartments but as a unified intelligence. Direction C is not the average of A and B — it is what A and B produce when they operate on the same problem at the same time. The visual result is a brand that is trusted because it is precise and desired because it is considered. That combination is rare. That rarity is the competitive advantage.

The second reason Direction C wins: it has the most scalable vocabulary. As Verk grows, adds services, builds case studies, and potentially enters international markets, Direction C can hold all of that. Direction A would have to evolve toward Direction C to remain interesting. Direction B would have to evolve toward Direction C to remain trustworthy. Direction C is where they both end up.

## Part 4 — Hero Direction Lock

Five original concepts. None borrowed from any reference discussed in previous documents.

---

### Hero Concept 1 — The Density Before the Signal

**Concept:**
The hero is made of compressed information — an extremely dense grid of small, low-opacity elements: coordinates, measurements, labels, arrows — all overlapping, all grey, all illegible. Not a decoration. The density is real: it represents the accumulated noise of a business that doesn't yet have a system. The state before Verk arrives.

Then, in the center of this density, the headline appears in its full weight — black, sharp, perfectly legible. Clean. Around the headline, the density retreats imperceptibly. The words arrive in clarity. Everything else recedes.

**Emotional impact:**
Recognition. The density is familiar to every business owner who has spent a Friday afternoon trying to make sense of 200 unread WhatsApp messages. The headline arriving in clarity is the promise of Verk.

**Visual language:**
Two registers simultaneously: noise and signal. The contrast is not light/dark — it is legible/illegible. The hero communicates through information overload and its resolution.

**Motion language:**
The density is already present when the page loads — it is the state the world is in. The headline assembles from left to right, one word at a time, each word at full opacity from the moment it appears, no fade. The density does not disappear — it simply becomes less visible as attention finds the headline. Like focus adjusting, not like things moving.

**Relation to La Obra:**
Every obra begins in apparent disorder — the site before the first mark, the canvas before the first stroke, the codebase before the first architecture decision. This hero is that moment.

**Feasibility:**
High. The density grid is an SVG or CSS grid rendered at low opacity. The headline entrance is a sequenced reveal. No heavy libraries required.

---

### Hero Concept 2 — Línea de Corte

**Concept:**
A single vertical line runs from the top of the viewport to the bottom, positioned slightly left of center. To the left of the line: the world as it currently is for the client — a slow site rendered in desaturated color, barely visible at 20% opacity. To the right of the line: the world Verk builds — clean, fast, operational, in full color, in Verk's visual language. The headline floats at the line, anchored to it, readable from both sides.

The line moves. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, it drifts to the right — the Verk world growing, the before-world shrinking — over a 30-second loop. Not a dramatic transition. A slow, confident displacement.

**Emotional impact:**
Before and after made spatial, not temporal. The viewer understands the transformation not through a case study but through a single frame. The implied promise: this is what happens when Verk arrives.

**Visual language:**
Division. The line is the product. What Verk builds is the cut — the separation between a business that loses leads and one that doesn't.

**Motion language:**
The slow drift of the line — 30 seconds to move approximately 15% of the viewport. This is ambient motion: the kind that registers in peripheral vision as "something is changing" without demanding direct attention. On hover: the line freezes. The world on each side becomes momentarily more visible, as if the comparison can be examined.

**Relation to La Obra:**
The cut is fundamental to both construction (the cut in stone, the section drawing) and creation (the film edit). La Línea de Corte is the obra's first gesture.

**Feasibility:**
Medium-high. Two overlapping versions of content, a clip-path driven by the line position. Animation is CSS or minimal JS. Feasible in V1, but requires careful performance management.

---

### Hero Concept 3 — El Plano que Respira

**Concept:**
The hero background is a technical drawing — an actual, complete architectural or system floor plan, rendered in extremely fine lines at very low opacity on the cream background. Not a generic grid. A real plan: the plan of a system. The footprint of an automation workflow, drawn as if it were a building. Rooms that are actually process stages. Corridors that are actually data flows. Windows that are actually integration points.

The headline sits above this plan. The CTAs sit below. The plan itself breathes — an extremely slow zoom, 0.5% scale increase over 20 seconds, creating the sense that the system is alive and growing.

**Emotional impact:**
The feeling of a document you almost don't notice, which upon closer inspection contains a complete world. The plan rewards attention. A visitor who glances at the hero sees a clean headline. A visitor who looks carefully sees the anatomy of a system.

**Visual language:**
The architectural drawing tradition — technical precision that is also aesthetically distinctive. The plan communicates: Verk designs before it builds. The obra was planned.

**Motion language:**
The breath — almost imperceptible growth of the plan behind the text. The plan never moves faster than the rate at which a plant grows. It is the slowest thing on the page.

**Relation to La Obra:**
Every obra begins with a plano. The site plan precedes the construction. The blueprint precedes the building. The hero shows Verk's work before it becomes visible.

**Feasibility:**
Medium. The plan must be designed once as a detailed SVG. The breathing animation is a CSS transform scale. The challenge is designing the plan itself with sufficient specificity and beauty.

---

### Hero Concept 4 — La Primera Decisión

**Concept:**
The hero is almost empty. An expanse of cream. Near the center-left: the isotipo, already present, as if it has been there for some time. Below it, after a long pause: the headline appears, one line at a time, with genuine weight between each line. Then the subheadline. Then the CTAs. Each element arrives only after the previous one has been fully read — not in animation terms, but in time terms. The pacing is set so that a normal reader will finish reading the headline just as the subheadline begins.

The page does not rush to fill itself. It builds at the pace of reading.

**Emotional impact:**
The quality of a person who is not trying to impress you. The confidence of someone who says one thing at a time and waits for it to land before saying the next. In a world of heroes that explode onto the screen with simultaneously appearing elements and video backgrounds and complex animations, this hero communicates: we know what we're doing, and we're in no hurry to prove it.

**Visual language:**
Subtraction as method. The hero's power is in what it does not have: no video, no gradient, no complex layout, no multiple simultaneous motions. Just the right words, in the right order, arriving at the right pace.

**Motion language:**
Sequential reveal at reading speed. Each element transitions from opacity 0 to 1 over 400ms, expo out. Delay between elements: approximately 1200ms — enough time to read, not enough time to wonder if something is broken.

**Relation to La Obra:**
Every obra begins with the decision to make the first mark. This hero is that decision — deliberate, unhurried, irrevocable.

**Feasibility:**
Highest. No dependencies beyond CSS transitions and a brief setTimeout sequence. Extremely performant, zero risk of browser incompatibility.

---

### Hero Concept 5 — Dos Capas, Una Superficie

**Concept:**
The hero exists on two transparent layers simultaneously. Layer one — foreground: the wordmark, headline, subheadline, CTAs — all in full opacity, in black. Layer two — background: the isotipo at very large scale (filling most of the viewport), rendered in extremely low-opacity violet, like a watermark in architectural vellum. The background layer moves at 30% of the scroll speed — parallax that creates depth without spectacle.

As the user scrolls past the hero into the first section, the isotipo layer stays visible behind the next section, gradually fading as the section content asserts itself. The transition is not a cut — it's the background layer being replaced. The isotipo was already there when the section arrived.

**Emotional impact:**
Depth. The perception that there is always more underneath — another layer, another system, another level of the obra — even when the surface looks simple.

**Visual language:**
Overlay and transparency — the visual language of vellum, tracing paper, architectural overlays. One drawing placed over another, each visible through the other. The technique used to study how a building's layers relate to each other: structure, electrical, plumbing, finishes.

**Motion language:**
The parallax between layers. The foreground content scrolls at normal speed. The isotipo background scrolls slower — creating the sense that the foreground is surface and the background is foundation.

**Relation to La Obra:**
La Obra has layers. The foundation, the structure, the systems, the finish. This hero makes those layers visible without explaining them.

**Feasibility:**
Medium. The parallax requires careful implementation to avoid jank on mobile — the background layer should be fixed on mobile devices, parallax only on desktop.

---

### Hero Selection: Concept 4 — La Primera Decisión

**For V1.**

Concept 4 is chosen as the V1 hero not because it is the most spectacular. Because it is the most honest, and in a launch state, honesty is more valuable than spectacle.

The risk of the other concepts: they require production assets that do not yet exist (the plano in Concept 3, the before-state content in Concept 2) or require technical investment that could delay launch and introduce performance risk (Concept 5's parallax on mobile, Concept 1's density grid management).

Concept 4 can be built in one day and will look exactly as intended on every device, in every browser, at every connection speed.

But the deeper reason: Concept 4 is the most different from what exists in the market. Not because it is the most elaborate — because it is the most restrained. In a landscape of hero sections that arrive with five simultaneous animations, a hero that fills itself at reading speed feels radical. It communicates: we don't need to perform confidence. We have it.

**Concepts 1 and 5 should be documented as future hero explorations for V2**, when Verk's visual assets are more developed and when performance testing can be done on the animation systems.

## Part 5 — Isotipo System Lock

**Final symbolic meaning:**

The isotipo is two elements in the moment of passing each other. Not yet arrived. Not departed. Passing. This is Verk's position: always at the point of transformation, the moment between the before and the after. The gap between the two shapes is not empty — it is the product. What happens in that gap is what Verk builds.

**Final emotional meaning:**

Confidence in motion. Not speed — direction. The shapes are not rushing. They know where they are going. The implied trajectory is clear and the shapes are fully committed to it. This is the emotional register of every sistem Verk builds: not frantic, not static. Deliberate and moving.

**Final structural meaning:**

Two load-bearing elements at the point of dynamic equilibrium. In structural engineering, dynamic equilibrium is the state where forces in motion are balanced — not at rest, but in balance. The isotipo represents this: two systems in operation, each carrying its load, together creating stability through motion rather than through stillness.

**Final motion meaning:**

The shapes always move along their implied trajectory. Upper shape: up and to the right. Lower shape: down and to the right. They are always going somewhere. When animated, they move in this direction and return to this direction. They do not rotate. They do not bounce. They travel.

### Isotipo Behaviors

**Hero:**
The isotipo in the hero appears at full scale in violet at 8% opacity behind the headline, already present when the page loads. It does not animate on entrance — it was there. As the headline and CTAs arrive in sequence, the isotipo remains unchanged. It is the foundation. The surface content is built on top of it.

**Navigation:**
The isotipo in the nav is the wordmark context: the simple logo (wordmark only) appears in the nav for most pages. The isotipo alone appears only when the brand is operating independently of text — in the favicon, in social contexts, in loading states. In the nav, the logo has 28px clear space on each side from the nav pill edges. When the mobile menu opens, the isotipo appears large and centered on the overlay, in cream on the dark background.

**Dividers:**
A single shape (one of the two, never both in the same divider) appears at the transition between sections. The shape is positioned flush to one edge of the viewport — left edge for light-to-dark transitions, right edge for dark-to-light transitions. The shape is the transition color (if transitioning to a dark section, the shape is dark). This means the section transition is not a line — it is a shape edge. The geometric mark of the obra, used structurally.

**Cards:**
The isotipo can appear as a very large background element within project cards — at 6–8% opacity, the shapes occupying the lower-right quadrant of the card. They should only be visible if the viewer looks for them. They should not compete with the card's content image.

**Loaders:**
If a loading state is required (form submission, page fetch), the isotipo animates as follows: the two shapes start at their correct positions. The upper shape rises 12px and returns. The lower shape descends 12px and returns. They alternate: one goes, the other stays, then the reverse. Timing: 600ms per cycle, expo in-out. This is the isotipo breathing — the obra in a holding pattern, not an apology for the wait.

**Transitions:**
During View Transitions between pages, the isotipo shape appears briefly at the point of transition — a 200ms flash of one shape sliding horizontally across the viewport, from left edge to right edge, synchronized with the page exit animation. This is the cut mark — the editing mark at the moment of transition.

**Hover states:**
When the logo (wordmark + shapes) is hovered, the shapes drift 2px along their implied trajectory (upper: 2px up-right, lower: 2px down-right) over 300ms, then return over 500ms. The movement is so small it is felt before it is seen. It communicates: the shapes are alive. They would continue moving if you released them.

**Background systems:**
At 4% opacity, the shapes tiled at a 48px grid create a micro-pattern for dark sections that need texture without imagery. At 10% opacity, a single shape at 400px+ scale creates a macro-background for editorial sections. At 60% opacity in the accent color, the shape becomes a full section visual element — a graphic device, not a background treatment.

**Masks:**
Project images can be clipped to the silhouette of a single isotipo shape. The image is visible only through the shape. This creates an immediate visual signature: the moment you see an image inside those specific angles, you know it is Verk's work.

**Frames:**
Two shapes, positioned in opposite corners (one upper-left, one lower-right), can create a visual frame around content. The frame does not close — the shapes do not touch. They suggest the boundaries of a composition without containing it.

## Part 6 — Typography Lock

### The Challenge

Geist was the assumption. It is challenged here.

**The problem with Geist:**
Geist was designed by Vercel for Vercel's product documentation and marketing. It is excellent for that purpose. It is also permanently associated with that purpose. Any designer who knows Vercel — and the audience Verk is building toward will eventually know Vercel — will feel Geist as a reference, not a choice. A brand identity should not evoke another company's design system the moment it appears.

**The problem with neutrality:**
Every grotesque used in the "serious design studio" space — Suisse, Aktiv, Neue Haas — has the same problem: it communicates "we are a design studio that knows what good typography looks like." This is not wrong. It is also not interesting. The market for neutral-grotesque-using design studios is saturated. Verk's typography should work harder.

**The problem with personality:**
Editorial New, Canela, or any high-contrast serif would give Verk personality — but the wrong kind. Serifs in the primary role make Verk feel literary, retrospective, cultural in the Fine Arts sense. Verk builds CRM integrations at midnight. The serif cannot hold that without incongruity.

### The Solution

**A grotesque that is genuinely unusual at display scale.**

The answer is **Neue Haas Grotesk Display** — not as a "safe Swiss grotesque" choice, but as the original source material for Helvetica, used in a way that Helvetica was never used: at extreme scale, with extremely tight tracking, treated as a structural element rather than a typographic convention.

The difference between Neue Haas Grotesk Display used conventionally and used as Verk's type system is entirely in the application rules. The same typeface can feel generic or extraordinary depending on how it is deployed.

**Why this works for Verk:**

At very large display sizes with tracking at -0.04em to -0.06em, Neue Haas Grotesk Display creates letterforms that feel drawn, not set. The details of the letterforms — the specific quality of the terminals, the optical corrections — become visible at scale and read as craft, not default. Helvetica has the same qualities but none of the freshness, because Helvetica has been used for everything. Neue Haas Grotesk retains those qualities without the baggage.

### The Complete System

**Display (headlines, hero, section titles):**
Neue Haas Grotesk Display, Bold (700) to Black (900), negative tracking (-0.03em to -0.06em), line-height 1.05 to 1.15. At sizes 48px and above, the type becomes the primary visual event on the page — not decorated with anything else, not competing with imagery at the same scale.

**Text (body, descriptions, extended copy):**
Neue Haas Grotesk Text, Regular (400) to Medium (500), tracking 0 at body size, line-height 1.65. The Text cut is optically corrected for smaller sizes — the same family but a different tool. It reads without effort.

**Technical (numbers, metrics, code, labels):**
Geist Mono, Regular (400). This is the one place Geist is correct — not as the primary system, but as the technical register. Numbers in a CTA ("24 horas"), dates, technical terms, and any data output should appear in Geist Mono. This creates a deliberate contrast: the obra (creative, structural, in Neue Haas) and the system (technical, data, in Geist Mono) are visually distinct.

**Section labels:**
Neue Haas Grotesk Display, Medium (500), 10–11px, letter-spacing 0.16em, uppercase. The label is as tracked as the display is tight. Maximum contrast between the smallest text and the largest text, through spacing rather than decoration.

## Part 7 — Color Lock

### The Challenge

The current theory assigns: Violet = system, Lime = signal. This is challenged.

**Challenge 1: Is violet honest?**
Violet appears in the isotipo standalone — but in the full logo, the shapes are lime. Which one is the true identity color? If there is an inconsistency in the logo system, it should be resolved, not elaborated.

**Resolution:** The inconsistency in the logo is an asset. The two shapes *change color* because color is their most expressive quality — they are not constrained to one reading. Lime and violet are both real. They are not interchangeable. They are the same geometry in different states.

**Challenge 2: Is the lime too aggressive?**
Lime is a high-visibility color. In the wrong context — as decoration, as background, as ambient color — it becomes aggressive. Should it be softened?

**Resolution:** No. The aggression is the point. Lime must be rare enough that when it appears, it commands attention. If it were softened, it would stop being lime and start being yellow-green, which has none of the same urgency. Keep it exactly as designed. Use it less, not more softly.

**Challenge 3: Is the cream warm enough to carry the brand?**
The cream is both a background and a material. In Direction A, it is construction material. In Direction C, it is the surface of the obra. The specific value matters.

**Resolution:** The cream is locked at #F0EEE9. Not warmer (becomes yellowed, reads as aged). Not cooler (drifts toward white, loses the material quality). Not darker (loses the distinction from card backgrounds). The cream is the paper on which everything else is drawn.

### Final Color Lock

**Ink — #0F0F0F:**
Not pure black. The specific near-black with a barely perceptible warmth that prevents it from reading as RGB absolute black (which looks digital and inert) while maintaining maximum contrast. Ink is the structural color: all body text, all headlines, all structural elements. The obra in black.

**Cream — #F0EEE9:**
The material surface. Every light section. Every card background on light sections. The material on which the obra is drawn. Not white. Not grey. Warm and specific.

**Lime — #BFEA00:**
The action color. Appears only when the user can do something. Primary CTA buttons, active nav elements, interactive state indicators. Never as decoration. Never in body copy. Never as a background color. Lime is the signal that the system is waiting for you. It should appear approximately 3–5 times on a full page. Never more.

**Violet — #6B6AF4:**
The intelligence color. Appears when Verk's technical capabilities are the subject: technology labels, AI references, the isotipo in standalone form, process indicators, automation diagrams. Calm, deep, not urgent. If lime says "do something now," violet says "something is already happening."

**Dark surface — #111111:**
For dark sections. Not as dark as ink, but dark enough that text reads as cream against it without difficulty. The gap between ink and dark surface allows dark-section card elements to be slightly lighter (#1A1A1A to #1E1E1E) without losing the dark-section reading.

**Greys — for information hierarchy:**
`#8C8C8C` for secondary text (captions, metadata, section labels on light backgrounds)
`#404040` for mid-weight information on light backgrounds
`#B3B3B3` for secondary text on dark backgrounds

### How Color Behaves

**Color announces what is happening, not who Verk is.**

This is the key distinction between using color as decoration (generic) and using color as communication (Verk). Each section's color tells the viewer what mode they are in:

- Cream + black: structural mode. Reading, understanding, orienting.
- Dark + cream: depth mode. The obra's interior. Process, method, intelligence.
- Dark + lime: action mode. A decision is available. The system is ready.
- Any + violet: technical intelligence mode. The layer beneath the surface.

A viewer should be able to read Verk's color system without ever having been told about it. The behavior makes itself legible.

## Part 8 — Motion Lock

### The Question

If Verk were a person moving through a room, how would it move?

Not as an analogy. As the actual answer, which will then be translated into motion principles.

### The Person

The person moves like a senior architect making their first inspection of a completed obra. They have been here many times during construction. They know where every beam is, how every junction was made. Now they are walking through the finished space for the first time — checking, confirming, evaluating.

They do not rush. The obra is finished. Rushing would be impatience, and impatience in this context would be a failure to honor the work. They move deliberately — each step has a reason. They stop in doorways. They run one hand along a surface. They crouch to examine a detail at floor level. They stand in the center of a room and simply look.

When they DO move faster, it is not urgency — it is efficiency. They know what they're looking for and they know where it is. The speed is not anxiety. It is competence.

They are not performing for anyone in the room. The inspection is not for show. It is for confirmation.

### The Translation

**Overall tempo:** Moderate. Not slow enough to feel precious or self-congratulatory. Not fast enough to feel restless or anxious. The tempo of someone who is working, not performing.

**Entrances:** Elements enter from the direction they would structurally come from. Content that exists below the current view enters from below. Content that exists to the side enters from the side. Nothing enters from an arbitrary direction. The motion respects the logic of the page.

**Holds:** After an element arrives, it holds — a beat of stillness before the next event. Approximately 80ms on fast sequences, up to 400ms on intentional pauses. The hold is where attention reads. Removing holds creates anxiety. Extending them creates respect.

**Exits:** Elements rarely exit — they are usually replaced. When something leaves, it exits confidently: not a fade (which is uncertain) but a slide in its direction of origin, confirming where it came from.

**Scale:** Motion events are small. The headline does not fly across the viewport. It arrives 32px from its final position, settles. The 32px is enough to communicate arrival. More would be performance.

**Easing:** Out-expo as the default — decelerating motion that feels like something with mass. The element has traveled from somewhere and is arriving here. Spring easing for interactive states (hover, active) — the slight overshoot and return of a physical response.

**The one exception:** The marquee is the only continuous motion. It moves at a pace that can be read while scrolling, approximately 60px per second. Fast enough to feel active. Slow enough to be legible. Pausing on hover.

**What Verk's motion never does:**
- Loop aimlessly (motion without purpose)
- Bounce beyond its overshoot (spring easing must be subtle: maximum 3% overshoot)
- Accelerate into its final position (ease-in at arrival creates urgency that doesn't belong)
- Move multiple elements in exactly the same direction at exactly the same time (stagger is structural, not decorative)
- Acknowledge the cursor with an aggressive response (cursor interactions are subtle, not dramatic)

## Part 9 — The Only Verk Test

Screenshots with the logo removed and text removed. What remains that is unmistakably Verk?

1. **That cream.** `#F0EEE9` is not any cream. It is a specific warmth that no site in the target market uses. It reads as a material decision, not a default.

2. **The two parallelogram shapes** — as divider, background element, mask, or transition mark. At any scale, in any color, the geometry is specific enough to be owned.

3. **The noise grain on dark sections** — present at 3–4% opacity. Invisible at a glance. If you remove it, something is missing. Its purpose is to give dark sections tactile presence rather than digital flatness.

4. **Headlines with no punctuation.** Not "Construimos sistemas." — "Construimos sistemas" with nothing after. The decision to end a statement without punctuation is a style decision that reads as confident, not casual.

5. **The section label — 11px, uppercase, #8C8C8C, tracked wide — above every headline.** The label is always above. Always this size. Always this tracking. It signals: someone designed this carefully.

6. **The lime cursor** — a lime dot that enlarges to a 32px ring on hover. Not the default pointer. The lime cursor is simultaneously a brand element and a functional affordance.

7. **The generous gap between the label and the headline** — approximately 20–24px. Standard design would close this gap. Verk leaves it. The gap is what makes both elements visible as separate decisions.

8. **The dark section darkness** — `#111111`, not `#000000`. The specific near-black with warmth. Pure black on a screen reads as digital absence. `#111111` reads as material presence.

9. **Geist Mono for all numbers** — any metric, percentage, date, or technical term in the body uses Geist Mono. The visual interruption of a mono typeface inside a grotesque body creates a recognizable rhythm.

10. **The wordmark is always lowercase** — even as the first element on a page, even in a formal context. "verk" never becomes "Verk." The lowercase is a commitment.

11. **The pill navigation, centered, dark, floating** — at the top of every page. Not left-aligned. Not edge-to-edge. Centered, compact, with the CTA in lime to the right of the links.

12. **The lime CTA button, always pill-shaped** — never rectangular, never with a border-only treatment. The lime pill is the conversion element. It is consistent enough across every page that a returning visitor knows it immediately.

13. **One section per page that is almost empty** — a headline on a cream background with very little else. The negative space is intentional and readable as intentional.

14. **Card borders at exactly 1px** — not 2px, not a shadow, not a background fill. The hairline border. A structural decision.

15. **The footer headline as a statement, not an invitation** — the footer always opens with a declarative sentence. Not "Contact us" or "Get in touch." A sentence that makes a claim.

16. **Images either full-bleed or masked in the isotipo shape** — the in-between state (partial width, floating) does not exist. Every image is either part of the structure or contained by the brand mark.

17. **The isotipo at large scale in at least one section** — not small, not as a subtle texture. Large, confident, owning the section. The marca visible at a scale that demands acknowledgment.

18. **No gradient as background** — color changes between sections are decided and complete. The jump from cream to dark is not eased. There are no gradient-background sections.

19. **The lime used exactly as many times as needed and not once more** — a page with five lime elements feels like a page where the action color has been diluted. Verk pages have three lime elements at most: the nav CTA, the main CTA, and one interactive indicator. The rarity makes each appearance command attention.

20. **The "before" present somewhere** — a screenshot of a chaotic WhatsApp thread, a slow-loading site bar, a spreadsheet with 500 rows. The problem is always referenced, because the problem is the reason Verk exists.

## Part 10 — The Brand Rules

**These are non-negotiable. They do not bend to client requests, project deadlines, or aesthetic trends.**

---

**Rule 1:**
The cream is never pure white. If the background of any Verk material becomes `#FFFFFF`, it has become generic. The specific warmth of `#F0EEE9` is what separates Verk's light surfaces from every other site in the market. Guard it.

**Rule 2:**
Lime appears only where the user can take action. It is never used as a text color, a border color, a background fill, or a decorative accent. When lime appears, it means: something is waiting for you. If lime appears where nothing is waiting, its signal is lost permanently.

**Rule 3:**
The isotipo shapes do not rotate. Their implied trajectory is fixed by their angle. Rotating them changes the direction of the implied motion and contradicts the symbolic meaning. An isotipo that rotates is no longer going somewhere — it is spinning in place.

**Rule 4:**
No headline is centered on a dark section. Dark sections use left-aligned text. Centering on dark creates a formal symmetry that belongs to invitations and awards ceremonies, not to systems builders. Flush left is a decision about where authority resides.

**Rule 5:**
Motion never completes in less than 300ms. Below 300ms, motion reads as a glitch, not a choice. The minimum duration for any intentional animation is 300ms. Most will be 500–700ms. Nothing that carries meaning rushes.

**Rule 6:**
Every section of the website has a section label. No section begins directly with a headline. The label is not optional decoration — it is the structural connector between the navigation system and the content system. Without the label, the user cannot determine their position in the obra.

**Rule 7:**
The wordmark is always "verk" — lowercase v. This applies in the headline of a press mention, in the first word of a paragraph, in a legal document. The brand makes this decision once and enforces it everywhere. A capital V is a different brand.

**Rule 8:**
No gradient background appears anywhere on the site. Color is a decision, not a transition. If two colors need to coexist in the same section, they are layered (an element of one color on a background of another). They are not blended. Gradients communicate uncertainty about color. Verk is not uncertain.

**Rule 9:**
A service is never described in more than two sentences anywhere outside a dedicated service page. A service card, a footer service link, a homepage service section — all are limited to two sentences. Concision is a value of the brand, not a space constraint.

**Rule 10:**
The obra is always presented as the result of a decision, not a template. Every visual element on the site should be explainable as a choice made for a reason. If an element cannot be defended in one sentence, it should be removed. The obra has no unexplained parts.
